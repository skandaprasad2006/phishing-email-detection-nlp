Traditional rule- based filters struggle to adapt to evolving attack strategies, mo- tivating a data-driven approach. This paper presents a phishing email detection system that combines TF-IDF feature extraction with Random Forest and Logistic Regression classifiers. The system was trained and evaluated on the CEAS 08 dataset from Kaggle, which contains approximately 18,000 labelled messages. INTRODUCTION Email is one of the most widely used channels for pro- fessional and personal communication, making it a persistent target for cyberattacks. Among these threats, phishing stands out for both its frequency and its impact. A phishing attack in- volves a fraudulent message that impersonates a trusted entity, such as a bank or an institution, in order to trick the recipient into disclosing credentials or clicking a malicious link. The Anti-Phishing Working Group (APWG) reported more than one million unique phishing incidents in a single quarter of 2023 [1], and the volume of such attacks has continued to increase. The financial and operational consequences for organizations can include data breaches, service disruption, and loss of user trust [2]. Most organizations currently rely on rule-based email filters that maintain blacklists of known malicious domains and flagged keywords. These systems provide a basic level of protection but cannot adapt to new threats. Attackers routinely bypass them by registering new domains, rephrasing content, or substituting characters with visually similar Unicode alter- natives to evade keyword matching [3]. Because every new evasion technique must be manually identified and added to the rule set, defenders are perpetually reacting to attacks rather than anticipating them [4]. Machine learning, and Natural Language Processing (NLP) in particular, addresses this limitation by learning discriminat- ing patterns directly from labelled data. Phishing emails con- sistently rely on specific language patterns: urgency phrases, authority impersonation, and prompts to verify account details. TF-IDF feature extraction captures this term-level regularity and feeds it to classical classifiers without the computational overhead of large neural models [5], [6], making NLP-based detection both effective and practical on standard hardware. This paper presents an end-to-end phishing detection pipeline implemented in Python on Google Colab. The system preprocesses raw email body text, extracts TF-IDF features, and evaluates two classifiers, Random Forest and Logistic Re- gression, on the CEAS 08 dataset from Kaggle. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section II reviews related work; Section III describes the proposed system; Section IV covers implementation details; Section V presents and dis- cusses the results; and Section VI concludes. II. LITERATURE SURVEY Phishing email detection has been studied extensively using methods ranging from classical machine learning to deep learning and large language models. Early work established TF-IDF combined with Naive Bayes or Support Vector Ma- chines (SVM) as a competitive baseline. Kumar and Singh [3] compared four classifiers, Naive Bayes, SVM, Decision Tree, and Random Forest, under identical conditions and found that Random Forest achieved the highest accuracy at 95.2%. Their ablation study further showed that omitting preprocessing steps such as stopword removal or HTML stripping measurably reduced performance across all models, directly informing the design of our pipeline. Transformer-based methods have since attracted consid- erable interest. Rahman et al. [5] fine-tuned BERT on a phishing corpus and reported 97.4% accuracy by exploiting context-sensitive word representations. Chen and Wang [7] demonstrated that RoBERTa fine-tuned on as few as 1,000 labelled examples achieves 91.3%, making transformer-based detection viable even with limited training data. Al-Subaihin and Sarro [10] explored zero-shot detection using GPT-4 and found that carefully crafted prompts can reach approximately 95% accuracy, although prompt injection from adversarially crafted emails remains an unresolved vulnerability. Other studies have approached the problem from different directions. Zhao et al. [15] proposed a CNN-LSTM model that processes email body text and embedded URLs simultane- ously, achieving 98.1% on 80,000 emails. Maneriker et al. [8] addressed adversarial obfuscation through a character-level neural network that correctly identified 99% of deliberately misspelled samples. Liao et al. [9] applied a Graph Neural Network to email thread analysis, flagging replies that break conversational flow with 96.7% accuracy on spear-phishing cases. Interpretability has also emerged as a practical concern. Pa- tel and Mehta [4] integrated SHAP attribution into a Random Forest model, enabling predictions to be traced to specific words and achieving 94.6% accuracy. Adebowale et al. [11] combined URL structural features with body content analysis, reporting 96.8% on an enterprise dataset. Gangavarapu et al. [12] fused email headers, metadata, body text, and URLs in a multimodal framework that reached 97.9% with robustness to class imbalance. Gupta et al. [2] surveyed over 60 detection papers and found a consistent pattern: most studies perform well under controlled benchmark conditions but have rarely been evalu- ated on live email traffic. Apruzzese et al. [13] addressed this by training on adversarially generated samples and observed improved robustness against novel attack patterns. Basit et al. [14] provided a taxonomy positioning NLP-based methods relative to URL-based and header-based approaches, clarifying where TF-IDF classifiers fit in the broader detection landscape. PROPOSED WORK A consistent observation across the literature is that phishing emails, regardless of how convincingly they are crafted, rely on a predictable set of vocabulary patterns. Phrases such as “verify immediately,” “your account has been suspended,” and “click here to confirm” appear frequently in phishing samples but rarely in legitimate correspondence. This term- level consistency is stable enough across campaigns for TF- IDF feature extraction to capture it reliably, without requiring a more computationally expensive model. The proposed system exploits this property: raw email text is cleaned through a preprocessing stage, converted into a TF-IDF feature vector, and classified by two independently trained classifiers, Ran- dom Forest and Logistic Regression, each producing a binary PHISHING or LEGITIMATE label. The modular design allows any stage to be updated independently. For example, the TF-IDF vectorizer could later be replaced with a contextual embedding layer without requiring changes to the downstream classifiers or preprocessing steps. When an email enters the pipeline, it passes through a text cleaning stage in which English stopwords are removed using NLTK, all tokens are lowercased, and the text is tokenized into individual words. The cleaned token list is passed to the TF-IDF vectorizer, which produces a fixed-length numerical vector. Each dimension corresponds to a vocabulary term, and its value reflects how discriminating that term is across the corpus. Words that appear frequently in phishing emails but rarely in legitimate mail, such as “verify,” “urgent,” “expire,” and “confirm,” naturally receive higher TF-IDF scores, making them influential features for classification. Both classifiers receive the same TF-IDF vector as input. Random Forest builds 100 decision trees, each trained on a randomly sampled data subset, and assigns the final class by majority vote, providing robustness to noisy or redundant features. Logistic Regression fits a linear decision boundary in the TF-IDF feature space and converts the resulting score to a probability, applying a 0.5 threshold to produce the binary label. Evaluating both classifiers under identical conditions enables a direct comparison of a nonlinear ensemble and a linear model on the same feature representation. Fig. 1. IMPLEMENTATION A. Dataset The system was trained and evaluated on the CEAS 08 phishing email dataset sourced from Kaggle. The dataset contains approximately 18,000 labelled email messages drawn from documented phishing campaigns and genuine corporate and personal correspondence. Each record includes the sender, receiver, date, subject, body, class label, and embedded URLs. Labels are binary: 0 for legitimate and 1 for phishing. The phishing subset covers common attack types including fake bank alerts, account-suspension notices, parcel delivery scams, prize-claim messages, and credential-harvesting attempts. The legitimate samples originate from anonymized corporate and personal inbox archives. The email body was used as the primary text input, with an 80/20 stratified train-test split applied to preserve the class distribution across both partitions. Software and Platform All experiments were implemented in Python 3 and exe- cuted in Google Colab, a cloud-based Jupyter environment requiring no local installation. Libraries used included: pandas for data loading and manipulation; scikit-learn for TF-IDF vectorization, model training, data splitting, and evaluation metrics; NLTK for stopword filtering and tokenization; and matplotlib for confusion matrix visualization. No GPU was required; the complete pipeline ran on a standard CPU runtime from data loading through to final evaluation. C. Preprocessing and Feature Extraction Email body text was cast to string type before processing to handle missing or null values. English stopwords were removed using NLTK and all text was lowercased to ensure consistent token representation. TF-IDF vectorization was then applied using scikit-learn’s TfidfVectorizer with built- in English stopword filtering, producing a sparse numerical feature matrix in which each term is weighted by its relative importance across the full corpus. The resulting matrix was used directly as input to both classifiers, with no additional dimensionality reduction applied. D. Constraints and Scope This work has several deliberate scope limitations. No deep learning architecture was used; the system relies entirely on TF-IDF features and classical machine learning classifiers. Emails are processed in batch mode from a static dataset rather than from a live mail server, so the system does not operate in real-time. The CEAS 08 dataset is a fixed benchmark, and models trained on it may require retraining as phishing techniques evolve. The system also does not examine email headers, sender metadata, or URL structure, all of which could contribute additional discriminating signals in future iterations. V. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION A. Discussion The close performance of Random Forest (99.27%) and Logistic Regression (99.23%) indicates that the discriminating signal in TF-IDF features is strong enough for a linear model to exploit almost as effectively as a nonlinear ensemble. Ran- dom Forest performs marginally better because its aggregation of 100 trees, each built on a random data and feature subset, provides greater robustness to correlated or noisy features. Logistic Regression occasionally misclassifies emails whose vocabulary spans both classes, as it must separate them with a single linear boundary rather than an aggregated vote. It is also important to look beyond accuracy when inter- preting these results. A naive classifier that assigned every email to the phishing class would achieve approximately 56% accuracy on this test set, given the class distribution. The 99.54% precision and 99.15% recall observed for Random Forest demonstrate that the model makes genuine discrimi- native decisions rather than benefiting from a class imbalance, Fig. 2. CONCLUSION This paper described a phishing email detection system built on TF-IDF feature extraction and two classical machine learning classifiers, Random Forest and Logistic Regression. Using the CEAS 08 dataset from Kaggle and implementing the full pipeline in Google Colab, the system achieved 99.27% accuracy, 99.54% precision, and 99.15% recall for Random Forest, and 99.23% accuracy for Logistic Regression, without any GPU hardware or deep learning framework. Results in- dicate that a well-structured NLP-based pipeline can achieve reliable detection performance on a labelled email benchmark. In practical terms, a system of this kind could be integrated as a server-side screening layer in an organization’s mail infrastructure, offering low-cost, interpretable detection that security teams can validate and act on. Future work will investigate contextual embeddings such as BERT or RoBERTa as replacements for TF-IDF, which may improve robustness against adversarially obfuscated content. Incorporating email headers, sender reputation, and URL structural features as supplementary inputs also represents a natural direction for extending the system. Evaluating performance on live email streams rather than static benchmarks would be the most informative test of its practical utility. REFERENCES [1] Anti-Phishing Working Group. (2024).
